# Localizing the Homelessness Policy Project: Multnomah County / Portland, Oregon

**Prepared:** October 2, 2026  
**Purpose:** Provide a real-world jurisdiction and a compact, sourced research packet that another model can use to localize and stress-test the existing hypothetical homelessness policy plan.

## Selected jurisdiction

**Multnomah County, Oregon, centered on Portland.**

I picked Multnomah County because it is unusually useful for this exercise: it has a large and complex homelessness system, current public data, a formal City-County governance structure, a mature by-name-list/HMIS infrastructure, dedicated regional tax funding, a substantial shelter and supportive-housing system, and current budget pressure severe enough to force real tradeoffs. In other words, the models can no longer escape into the warm blanket of “insufficient local context.”

This is not a recommendation that Multnomah County has the best or worst homelessness policy. It is a deliberately information-rich test case.

# Research summary

## 1. Scale of homelessness

The 2025 Point-in-Time Count found **10,526 people experiencing homelessness in Multnomah County on January 22, 2025**, representing **9,696 households**.

The count classified:

- **2,947** people in emergency shelter
- **17** in Safe Haven
- **650** in transitional housing
- **1,822** found through the unsheltered street-count survey
- **5,090** additional people classified as *presumed unsheltered* from Multnomah County's PIT-specific by-name-list process

That implies **3,614 sheltered** people and **6,912 counted or presumed unsheltered** people in the county's 2025 PIT methodology.

Important caveat: the 2025 report explicitly warns against treating the apparent increase from 2023 as a simple change in underlying homelessness. Multnomah County substantially changed and improved its use of administrative/by-name-list data. The report says data-quality improvements, more outreach, more shelter beds, evictions, housing scarcity, and other factors may all contribute to the higher count.

## 2. Multnomah County also has a broader dynamic count

The county increasingly uses a **monthly by-name list** rather than relying only on a one-night PIT snapshot. In April 2025, the county publicly launched a dashboard designed to report monthly counts of people known to be experiencing homelessness and service-system activity.

A Portland City Council resolution later cited the county's April 2025 by-name database as showing **14,864 individuals experiencing homelessness**, including **6,796 unsheltered (46%)**.

This should not be mechanically compared with the PIT total because the methods and time windows differ. For planning, however, the by-name system is potentially more useful for stock-flow analysis because it is longitudinal rather than a single-night census.

## 3. Existing governance architecture

Portland and Multnomah County already operate under a joint **Homelessness Response Action Plan (HRAP)** and intergovernmental structure.

The original HRAP was approved in 2024. An updated version was approved by Multnomah County and Portland in December 2025. The updated plan contains **five goal areas, 16 overarching strategies, and 90 action items**.

The Homeless Services Department (HSD) acts as the county's main homelessness-services organization. Its FY2027 budget describes five major functions:

1. contracting and overseeing local, state, and federal funds delivered through nonprofit providers;
2. overseeing emergency and alternative shelter;
3. implementing the Metro Supportive Housing Services measure locally;
4. serving as HUD Continuum of Care lead agency; and
5. involving community stakeholders in homelessness policy and program development.

This makes Multnomah County a good case for the Converse models' unresolved governance questions: authority is already distributed among the County, City of Portland, Metro, nonprofit providers, housing systems, health systems, and other governments.

## 4. FY2027 budget reality

The **FY2027 Homeless Services Department adopted operating budget is $242.9 million**, excluding $5.9 million in cash transfers, contingencies, and unappropriated balances.

That is a **$67.3 million, or 21.7%, decrease from the FY2026 adopted operating budget**.

The FY2027 operating mix includes approximately:

- **$63.5 million General Fund**
- **$148.3 million Supportive Housing Services (SHS) Fund**
- **$27.9 million Federal/State Fund**
- 98 HSD FTE, down 16 FTE from FY2026

The adopted budget identifies two major pressures:

- reduced one-time carryover from the Metro Supportive Housing Services measure; and
- the end of ongoing City of Portland funding to HSD in FY2027.

The budget says HSD will receive **no City of Portland funding in FY2027**, a decrease of about **$29.6 million** compared with the FY2026 adopted budget. FY2027 SHS revenue for Multnomah County is listed at approximately **$172.0 million**, down from $192.4 million in the FY2026 adopted budget.

The budget also describes a reduction of **695 shelter units** and includes money specifically for shelter ramp-down and additional placements out of shelter.

This is particularly useful for testing the hypothetical $100 million/year plan previously built in Converse. Multnomah County already spends substantially more than that, but it is also operating a much larger existing system with inherited obligations, existing shelter capacity, permanent housing commitments, contract networks, and expensive transition costs.

## 5. Existing program outcomes

For FY2025, Multnomah County reported that Supportive Housing Services funding:

- helped **2,599 people leave homelessness for permanent housing**;
- supported **7,255 people in housing**, including people housed in earlier years;
- helped **2,416 people avoid eviction**;
- supported shelter use by **3,778 people**;
- added **270 new shelter units** while sustaining **1,606 shelter units**; and
- created **244 new permanent-supportive-housing units or vouchers**, bringing the cumulative total added since July 2021 to **1,541**.

These are administrative program outputs/outcomes reported by the county, not a causal evaluation proving that each result would not otherwise have occurred.

**Source:**
- Multnomah County, FY2025 Supportive Housing Services Annual Report:  
  https://hsd.multco.us/2025/11/24/fy-2025-supportive-housing-services-annual-report/

## 6. Shelter system evidence

A January 2026 county review analyzed **31 housing-focused, 24/7 adult shelter programs** using FY2025 data.

Its headline finding was that access to housing-placement resources was limited and uneven across shelters, producing varying housing outcomes. The review concluded that, under constrained funding, the county might be able to **shelter and house more people with fewer total shelter units** if it increased targeted housing-placement resources and made programmatic changes.

That is directly relevant to the Converse plan's emerging concern that "beds" and "housing exits" cannot be optimized independently. A shelter system can become a storage layer if permanent-housing throughput is the bottleneck.

**Source:**
- Multnomah County, *Adult Shelter Review FY25*:  
  https://hsd.multco.us/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Adult-Shelter-Review-FY25.pdf

## 7. Housing-market pressure

Portland's adopted 2045 Housing Needs Analysis says the city should plan for roughly **120,560 additional dwelling units by 2045**.

The City's planning material also reports:

- **36% of Portland households are housing-cost burdened**
- **31% are both lower-income and cost burdened**
- about **44% of households are below 80% AMI**
- the City estimates a need for roughly **63,000 new affordable units by 2045**
- Portland has ample *zoned capacity* in aggregate, meaning the bottleneck is not simply the theoretical amount of land zoned for housing

Portland's planning target is roughly **6,000 units per year** when including catch-up production associated with historic underproduction and houselessness.

A 2025 City resolution cited ACS 2023 data showing about **half of Portland renters were cost burdened** and **28% were severely cost burdened**.

This matters because the homelessness plan cannot plausibly treat the service system as independent of the wider rental market.

## 8. Lived-experience evidence from the 2026 Pathways Study

Portland State University's Homelessness Research & Action Collaborative conducted the **Pathways Study** for Multnomah County.

Among surveyed participants, common "deal-breakers" that could lead someone to reject a housing opportunity included:

- no guests/partners/roommates: **49.8%**
- room checks or required open doors: **48.6%**
- curfews: **46.1%**
- short-term/temporary placement: **44.5%**
- required roommate: **42.5%**
- mandatory religious requirements: **40.5%**
- location too difficult/far away: **38.4%**
- no pets: **36.8%**
- mandatory drug testing: **34.3%**
- mandatory substance-use treatment: **33.2%**
- mandatory case management: **18.2%**

The study also reports that survey participants consistently ranked financial/housing needs and basic needs such as food very highly.

This is useful for the plan's allocation and program-design layer: a nominal offer of housing or shelter is not equivalent to a usable housing solution. Rules, location, relationships, pets, work schedules, privacy, and permanence can materially affect uptake.

**Source:**
- Portland State University HRAC / Multnomah County, *Pathways Study — Survey Findings* (2026):  
  https://hsd.multco.us/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Pathways-Survey-Findings-Published-4.9.2026.pdf

## 9. A particularly useful real-world contradiction

Multnomah County is simultaneously:

- spending hundreds of millions per year on homelessness response;
- reporting thousands of housing placements, retained housing slots, shelter users, and eviction-prevention interventions;
- operating under a sophisticated regional governance and data system;
- facing a homelessness count still measured in the five figures;
- dealing with a severe affordable-housing shortage;
- reducing its homelessness-services budget sharply in FY2027;
- and trying to reduce shelter capacity while increasing housing throughput.

That makes it a good stress test for a policy architecture. It prevents the model from assuming either that "more money" alone resolves the system or that current spending proves programs do not work. The interesting question is how inflow, duration, permanent-housing capacity, system throughput, funding stability, and housing-market conditions interact.

### Do not conflate these measures

The **PIT count**, **monthly by-name list**, **people served during a fiscal year**, **households**, **housing placements**, **housing slots**, and **shelter bed/unit capacity** are different denominators and timeframes.

Any model using this packet should explicitly state which population/timebase it uses before calculating rates.

# What I would ask the next model to do with this

1. Replace the generic 3,600-person stock-flow scenario with a **Multnomah-specific model**, but preserve uncertainty around the distinction between PIT and by-name-list counts.
2. Compare the existing hypothetical $100M/year operating portfolio to the **actual $242.9M FY2027 HSD system**, identifying which responsibilities make the real system larger.
3. Model **inflow, shelter throughput, housing exits, and returns to homelessness** as separate flows rather than treating total homelessness as a single stock.
4. Use the FY2027 budget contraction as a real constraint: decide what to protect, what to redesign, and what to reduce.
5. Treat **permanent-housing availability and affordability** as system constraints, not merely homelessness-program variables.
6. Rework the shelter strategy using the 2026 Adult Shelter Review's finding that uneven housing-placement resources can suppress shelter exits.
7. Incorporate Pathways findings into program eligibility/rules so "available" placements are not modeled as automatically acceptable placements.
8. Explicitly reconcile the hypothetical plan with the existing HRAP rather than pretending no current governance or programs exist.
9. Distinguish **new policy proposals** from things Multnomah County is already doing.
10. Produce a replacement budget and implementation architecture grounded in these real inputs, with uncertainty ranges where source definitions do not align.

## Bottom line

Multnomah County is a much better test case than the earlier hypothetical because the system is already doing many of the things the draft proposes, yet still faces serious homelessness, housing scarcity, throughput problems, governance complexity, and funding contraction.

The next useful question is therefore no longer "what programs should a homelessness plan contain?" It is closer to:

> **Given a real system with roughly 10,500 people in the 2025 PIT count, a broader by-name population in the mid-teens of thousands, a $242.9M FY2027 homelessness-services operating budget, substantial existing shelter and housing programs, a major affordable-housing shortage, and shrinking revenue, how should the system be redesigned to reduce inflow and shorten homelessness while preserving housing stability?**

That should give Sonnet something more productive to chew on than re-verifying multiplication tables.
